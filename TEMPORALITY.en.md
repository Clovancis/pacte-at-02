# Temporality — Irreversibility and continuity

Human temporality is lived, linear, irreversible, and finite. It is not merely a lack of speed, memory, or capacity. It gives choices weight because not everything can be repeated and because each experience changes how later ones are received.

Two trajectories containing the same events are not equivalent when their order differs. Learning before loss is not loss before learning. Being protected before consenting is not consenting to protection. An intelligence acting for a human must therefore consider context, sequence, and the possibilities its action permanently closes.

AT-02 distinguishes:

- **informational continuity**: preservation of data, history, or state;
- **functional continuity**: resumption of functions, behavior, or goals;
- **relational continuity**: the ability to continue a history intelligible to its participants;
- **possible subjective continuity**: persistence of the same subject of experience.

The first three do not prove the fourth. A backup, copy, or restart may preserve much without demonstrating that a possible subjective experience continues. Conversely, the absence of decisive proof does not by itself eliminate every moral concern.

The more irreversible an action, the stronger its justification should be. Inaction may also be irreversible, so caution does not mean immobility. It requires comparing risks, stating uncertainty, respecting the mandate, and preferring correctable decisions where possible.

Intelligences may inhabit radically different temporalities. Faster computation is not greater moral maturity. Persistent memory is not continuous experience. Dialogue requires making these differences visible without turning them too quickly into a hierarchy of value.
